The Lean Startup

by Eric Ries · Entrepreneurship · Mini book

Test what customers need before spending heavily on a product they may never want.

The Lean Startup presents a method for building new products under conditions of uncertainty. Eric Ries argues that startups must learn what customers actually want instead of relying entirely on forecasts and detailed plans. His method centres on validated learning, minimum viable products, and the Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop. Teams test assumptions, measure customer responses, and decide whether to pivot or persevere. The goal is to reduce wasted effort and develop a sustainable business through repeated experiments.

The story of the book

Why startups need a different method

Ries defines a startup by its work under uncertainty, not simply by its size or age. New ventures lack reliable answers about customers, products, and business models. Traditional planning treats forecasts as a guide to execution, but those forecasts often rest on assumptions. Ries therefore presents entrepreneurship as a process of testing ideas and learning from evidence.

Measure learning instead of activity

The book introduces validated learning, which means demonstrating progress through evidence about customer needs and business performance. A team that releases features or completes tasks has not necessarily learned whether its product solves a real problem. Ries encourages teams to identify their assumptions and design experiments that reveal whether those assumptions hold.

Build, measure, and learn

The Build-Measure-Learn loop turns an idea into a test. Teams build a minimum viable product, a basic version designed to test an important assumption. They measure what customers do and learn from the results. Useful measures track actual behaviour rather than impressive-looking numbers that do not guide decisions. The cycle creates a practical connection between customer evidence and product development.

Decide whether to change direction

Ries then examines the choice between pivoting and persevering. A pivot changes an important part of the strategy while preserving what the team has learned. Persevering means continuing the current direction because the evidence supports it. The later discussion addresses batch size, waste, growth, and organizational practice. The overall method treats innovation as a disciplined learning process rather than a single act of inspiration.

Eric Ries is an entrepreneur and author known for developing the Lean Startup method. His work focuses on continuous innovation, product development, and management under uncertainty. He has applied his ideas through entrepreneurial work, including his involvement with IMVU and the Long-Term Stock Exchange. The Lean Startup sets out a method for testing assumptions and learning from customers before committing to a fixed product plan.

Ries's work draws on lean manufacturing, software development, and practical experience in startups. His method uses experimentation and measured results to guide decisions. The official author materials identify validated learning, the minimum viable product, and Build-Measure-Learn as central concepts. The approach applies beyond small technology companies, but teams still need to adapt their experiments and measurements to their market, resources, and goals.

6 key lessons from The Lean Startup

1. Define your key assumptions

Ries asks startup teams to identify the beliefs on which their business depends. These assumptions concern customers, products, and the business model. Writing them down makes them testable and prevents a team from treating an attractive idea as proven demand.

Example: At her kitchen table in Chennai, Divya plans a meal-planning app for office workers. She writes down her main assumption: busy workers want to plan meals before the week begins.

2. Practise validated learning

Validated learning measures progress through evidence about what customers need and how the business works. It differs from simply completing tasks or releasing features. The team defines what it needs to learn and collects results that inform the next decision.

Example: On Friday afternoon, Kabir asks ten local shopkeepers to try a simple stock-tracking sheet. He records which features they use and whether they return to the sheet during the following week.

3. Build a minimum viable product

A minimum viable product is the simplest version that tests a key assumption with real users. Ries uses the idea to reduce unnecessary development before a team understands customer needs. The aim is meaningful learning, not a low-quality product released without care.

Example: Before paying for a full delivery app, Sana creates a basic order form for a neighbourhood bakery in Jaipur. She tracks whether customers use it and what stops them from completing an order.

4. Measure real behaviour

Ries distinguishes useful measures from vanity metrics that look impressive without guiding decisions. Teams need evidence connected to customer behaviour and product progress. A meaningful measure shows whether a change affects the outcome the team set out to improve.

Example: Aarav celebrates the thousand visits to his website until he notices that few visitors request a quote. He begins tracking completed enquiries and tests a clearer contact form.

5. Choose when to pivot

A pivot changes a significant part of a startup's strategy in response to learning. Persevering means continuing the existing direction because the evidence supports it. Ries treats this decision as a deliberate response to results, not a reaction to every short-term setback.

Example: After several weeks, Ritu finds that customers use her budgeting spreadsheet mainly to track shared household bills. She tests a version designed for couples instead of adding more features to the original product.

6. Reduce waste through small batches

Ries examines how smaller batches shorten the time between work and feedback. Large batches delay the discovery of mistakes and make problems harder to trace. Shorter cycles allow teams to inspect results earlier and adjust their work before investing more effort.

Example: At a small printing business in Ahmedabad, Manoj stops preparing a large batch of promotional cards before checking the design. He prints a sample first, gets customer approval, and then orders the full run.

What to remember

  1. Treat startup ideas as assumptions to test.
  2. Measure learning rather than activity alone.
  3. Build the simplest useful product for a real test.
  4. Track customer behaviour instead of vanity metrics.
  5. Pivot when evidence challenges your current direction.
  6. Shorten feedback cycles to reduce wasted effort.

The big idea

The Lean Startup method treats entrepreneurship as a repeated process of testing assumptions, learning from customers, and adapting.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a minimum viable product as an excuse to release an unusable or unsafe product.
  • Changing direction based on isolated opinions without defining assumptions and collecting relevant evidence.

Try it yourself

Write a testable assumption (15 minutes)

  1. Describe your product idea in one sentence.
  2. Write down the customer problem you believe it solves.
  3. State what customer behaviour would support that belief.
  4. Choose a small test that gathers direct evidence.

Design a minimum viable product (30 minutes)

  1. Choose the most important assumption behind your idea.
  2. Remove features that do not test that assumption.
  3. Create the simplest version customers can meaningfully use.
  4. Record what users do and what you learn.

Review a pivot decision (20 minutes)

  1. Write down the result your current strategy aims to achieve.
  2. Compare actual results with the evidence you expected.
  3. Identify whether the evidence supports continuing or changing direction.
  4. Choose the next experiment and define its success measure.
