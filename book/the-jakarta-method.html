The Jakarta Method

by Vincent Bevins · Politics & Society · Mini book

Explore how Cold War anti-communist campaigns shaped Indonesia and influenced political violence across other countries.

Vincent Bevins investigates Cold War anti-communist violence across the developing world. He focuses on the 1965 mass extermination of suspected leftists in Indonesia backed by American officials. Bevins demonstrates how the Indonesian killings became a deliberate global playbook against social reform movements throughout Latin America and Asia. The book examines the direct human toll of geopolitical containment strategies. Bevins argues that violent anti-communist campaigns built the modern global economic order.

The story of the book

A world divided by the Cold War

Bevins analyzes the international environment following the Second World War. Newly decolonized nations in the Global South sought independent development paths through the Non-Aligned Movement. Leaders in Washington and Moscow viewed these post-colonial struggles through a strict Cold War lens. Bevins details how American foreign policy prioritized anti-communist alignment above local democratic sovereignty and human rights.

Indonesia's violent turning point

The book documents the 1965 political upheaval in Indonesia, where military forces seized power following an aborted coup. General Suharto's army organized the mass extermination of approximately one million suspected communists, labor organizers, and peasant activists. American intelligence agencies provided communications gear, covert funds, and execution lists. The slaughter dismantled Indonesia's left and installed an authoritarian military regime.

A method that travelled

Bevins demonstrates how the Indonesian extermination campaign became an international blueprint for right-wing military regimes. Slogans referencing Jakarta appeared in Chile, Brazil, Argentina, and Guatemala to terrorize political reformers. Washington supported covert state terror networks like Operation Condor across Latin America. The book shows that anti-communist terror functioned as a deliberate, interconnected international program.

Remembering the people affected

The narrative shifts focus from high-level geopolitical strategy to the living testimony of survivors and displaced families. Decades of state-sponsored anti-communist terror silenced civic dissent and distorted democratic institutions across three continents. Bevins argues that modern global capitalism was forged through covert mass murder. The book asks readers to reckon with the human cost of Cold War hegemony.

About Vincent Bevins

Vincent Bevins is an American journalist and author who has reported on international politics, conflict, and social change. His reporting has included work in Brazil and other parts of Latin America, as well as coverage of political developments in different regions. His journalism focuses on how large political forces affect people's lives and how historical events shape present-day societies.

In The Jakarta Method, Bevins combines historical research with reporting and personal accounts to examine anti-communist violence during the Cold War. The book follows Indonesia while connecting its experience to political developments in other countries. Bevins argues that the Indonesian killings influenced wider anti-communist strategies and political thinking. The work has drawn attention to a history that many general accounts of the Cold War treat briefly, while its claims about international influence should be assessed alongside other historical research.

6 key lessons from The Jakarta Method

1. Foreign policy has human costs

Bevins asks readers to look beyond diplomatic goals and examine the people affected by international strategy. Policies described as security measures have serious consequences when local forces use them to justify violence. A complete assessment needs evidence about both political aims and human outcomes.

Example: At 8:30 AM at a university archive in Washington, a graduate researcher named Daniel analyzes declassified 1965 diplomatic cables. He compares official embassy memos with survivor testimonies documenting mass civilian detentions in rural Java.

2. Local politics still matters

The book connects international influence with local political conflicts rather than presenting countries as passive pieces on a global board. Domestic leaders, military organisations, and social movements make decisions within their own conditions. Understanding these actors explains why similar international pressures produce different outcomes.

Example: At 2:00 PM at a municipal council office in Surabaya, a political scientist named Maya studies 1960s regional records. She maps how domestic military rivalries and agrarian disputes interacted with superpower Cold War foreign funding.

3. Political labels are dangerous

Bevins examines how anti-communism became a justification for repression. Political labels are used to present a broad group as an enemy, making violence appear necessary to supporters. Readers should examine how accusations are defined and whether individuals are judged through evidence or collective suspicion.

Example: At 11:00 AM at a newsroom in Jakarta, an editor named Reza reviews historical radio transcripts. He notes that military broadcasters labeled ordinary peasant union members as dangerous subversive conspirators to justify sweeping extrajudicial arrests.

4. Mass violence leaves lasting effects

The book shows that political killings affect more than the people directly targeted. Families lose relatives, communities lose leaders, and public discussion is shaped by fear. These effects continue after the violence ends, influencing institutions and what people feel safe saying.

Example: At 4:30 PM at a community center in Bali, an oral historian named Wayan interviews elderly villagers. Families describe how decades of state-enforced political blacklisting prevented children of detained activists from securing public sector employment.

5. Historical memory shapes politics

Bevins examines how some histories of political violence are suppressed or given limited attention. What a society remembers influences how it understands its institutions and past decisions. Careful research, testimony, and public discussion help people assess claims that official accounts leave out.

Example: At 10:00 AM at the human rights memorial in Santiago, a museum curator named Carmen prepares an exhibit on Operation Condor. She displays correspondence between international military regimes alongside photographs of disappeared trade union organizers.

6. Check claims about international influence

The book argues that political methods travel across borders, but international connections require evidence. Similar events do not automatically prove direct coordination or imitation. Readers should distinguish documented relationships, shared ideas, and events that merely appear alike.

Example: At 3:30 PM at the national archives in Rio de Janeiro, a researcher named Lucas compares Brazilian police interrogation handbooks with American training files. He verifies documented financial transactions before publishing claims of bilateral operational coordination.

What to remember

  1. Cold War history includes the experiences of people outside superpower capitals.
  2. Anti-communist policy shaped political choices in many countries.
  3. Local leaders and institutions also influenced violent outcomes.
  4. Political labels are used to justify repression.
  5. Mass violence leaves lasting social and political effects.
  6. Claims about international influence need historical evidence.

The big idea

Bevins argues that anti-communist violence in Indonesia became a significant influence on political repression elsewhere and must be understood as part of Cold War history.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming that every local conflict was controlled entirely by foreign governments.
  • Treating similar events in different countries as proof of direct coordination without supporting evidence.
