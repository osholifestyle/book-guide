Tiny Habits

by BJ Fogg · Habits · Mini book

Start with a behavior small enough to do, attach it to a reliable daily prompt, and build change through repeated success.

BJ Fogg presents behavior change as a design problem rather than a test of character. His Behavior Model explains that motivation, ability, and a prompt work together when behavior occurs. Tiny Habits makes a new action easier by reducing it to a small step. You then attach it to an existing routine and celebrate completing it. The book also explains how to adjust unwanted behaviors and build supportive habits with other people.

The story of the book

Understand Behavior

Fogg begins by explaining that behavior depends on three elements: motivation, ability, and a prompt. His model describes how these elements work together at the moment an action occurs. A person may want to exercise but find the available routine too difficult. Another person may have enough motivation and ability but receive no reminder to begin. Looking at these conditions makes behavior easier to analyze. Instead of treating every failure as a personal weakness, the reader learns to examine what needs to change in the situation.

Make the Action Tiny

The method focuses on reducing the size of a desired behavior. Rather than demanding a complete workout, a person might begin with a very small movement. Rather than setting a large reading target, a reader might start with one sentence. The point is to reduce the effort required to begin. Fogg also encourages people to match behaviors with their actual interests and abilities. A small action is a starting point, not a limit on future progress. The behavior can grow as the person learns what works.

Attach a Prompt and Celebrate

A tiny action still needs a reliable reminder. Fogg's approach uses an existing routine as an anchor for a new behavior. The reader creates a clear recipe linking the familiar action to the desired step. Completing the action is followed by a celebration that creates a positive emotional response. Fogg treats that immediate feeling as an important part of the method. The purpose is to make success feel rewarding, not to claim that every celebration produces a measurable brain change or guarantees permanent learning.

Grow and Adjust

The later sections explain how tiny behaviors can develop, how unwanted habits can be approached systematically, and how people influence each other's changes. The method is flexible: a behavior that proves too difficult can be reduced or redesigned. A weak prompt can be replaced with a more reliable one. Supportive relationships can also make change easier to sustain. The book's central message is practical rather than dramatic. Design the behavior to fit real life, notice what happens, and adjust the conditions instead of relying on motivation alone.

About BJ Fogg

BJ Fogg is a behavioral scientist who founded the Behavior Design Lab at Stanford University. His work examines how behavior develops and how technology and other conditions influence human actions. He studied persuasive technology, an area concerned with how computers can affect attitudes and behavior. His academic work and teaching led to a broader approach he calls Behavior Design, which focuses on understanding behavior and creating practical ways to change it.

Fogg wrote Tiny Habits to present his method to a general audience. His official website describes years of research and experience coaching people through behavior change. The book connects his Behavior Model with small actions, existing routines, and positive emotion. Fogg has also taught behavior design to industry innovators and worked on methods for designing products that support healthier behavior. His approach emphasizes changing the conditions around an action instead of treating motivation or self-control as the only answer.

6 key lessons from Tiny Habits

1. Use the Behavior Model

When a behavior does not happen, examine motivation, ability, and the prompt. One of these conditions may be missing or insufficient. Adjust the design before blaming yourself. The model offers a way to investigate a specific action rather than judge your character.

Example: At 7 a.m. in Jaipur, Nisha wants to drink water but forgets. She places a glass beside her kettle so the morning tea routine reminds her.

2. Reduce the First Step

Make the first version of a new behavior small enough to begin with little effort. A tiny action removes some of the practical barriers that stop people from starting. Expand it later if you choose, but first make beginning manageable.

Example: At 6 p.m. in Hyderabad, Arjun finds a full workout intimidating. He puts on his trainers and walks outside for two minutes after work.

3. Attach the Habit to an Anchor

An existing routine can serve as a prompt for a new action. Write a clear recipe that states what happens first and what you will do next. Choose an anchor that already occurs reliably in your day.

Example: After brushing her teeth at 10 p.m. in Kochi, Priya writes one sentence in her journal. The familiar bedtime routine reminds her what to do.

4. Celebrate the Small Success

Notice completion immediately and choose a celebration that feels genuine. The point is to create a positive experience around the behavior. A celebration should suit you; forced enthusiasm is not necessary, and a particular gesture does not guarantee permanent habit formation.

Example: After clearing his desk at 5 p.m. in Surat, Mohan smiles and acknowledges the completed task before leaving work.

5. Choose a Behavior You Want

A plan is easier to follow when the behavior fits your interests and circumstances. Compare the action you think you should do with alternatives that meet the same goal. Choose a workable option instead of forcing a routine that does not suit you.

Example: At noon in Mysuru, Kavya wants more movement but dislikes running. She chooses a short walk through the garden after lunch.

6. Redesign Unwanted Behavior

Examine what prompts an unwanted routine and how difficult it is to resist. Change the surroundings, remove a trigger, or create an easier alternative. The goal is to alter the conditions supporting the behavior rather than depend only on guilt.

Example: At 9 p.m. in Chandigarh, Sameer keeps checking social media while studying. He leaves his phone in another room and places his textbook on the desk.

The big idea

Design small behaviors that fit your real circumstances, attach them to reliable prompts, and build on what works. The method favors practical adjustment over relying on motivation alone.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a new behavior that demands more effort than the available time, skills, or circumstances allow.
  • Using an unreliable reminder or ignoring the immediate experience of completing the action.

Try it yourself

Write Three Tiny Habit Recipes (15 minutes)

  1. Identify three routines you already perform reliably, such as brushing your teeth or making tea.
  2. Choose one small behavior you want to add to each routine.
  3. Write each recipe in the form: After I complete the existing routine, I will perform the tiny behavior.
  4. Read the recipes and reduce any action that still feels difficult to begin.

Find a Natural Celebration (5 minutes)

  1. Choose a tiny behavior and complete it once.
  2. Try a natural response such as smiling, acknowledging success, or making a small gesture.
  3. Notice which response feels genuine rather than forced.
  4. Use that response immediately after completing the behavior and observe how it feels.

Check the Ability Chain (15 minutes)

  1. Choose a behavior that you find difficult to perform consistently.
  2. Examine whether time, money, physical effort, mental effort, or fit with your routine creates a barrier.
  3. Choose the main obstacle and make one change that reduces it.
  4. Try the redesigned behavior and review whether it has become easier to perform.

Should you read Tiny Habits?

Read it if

Readers who struggle to start exercise, reading, planning, or other daily routines can use the book's small-step approach. It also suits people who want a structured way to examine why a behavior happens or fails to happen. Parents, teachers, managers, and product designers may find its focus on prompts and practical difficulty useful. The book is especially relevant to readers who prefer testing manageable changes over making large promises about future behavior.

You may skip it if

Readers who want a detailed academic textbook on neuroscience may prefer a research-focused work. People seeking an instant solution to a complex behavioral or medical problem should not expect tiny habits to replace professional guidance. The method requires observation and adjustment, and its results depend on the person and situation. Readers who want strict, demanding routines may also find its emphasis on small beginnings different from their preferred style.
