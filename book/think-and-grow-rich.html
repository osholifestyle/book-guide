Think and Grow Rich

by Napoleon Hill · Self-Improvement · Mini book

Clear goals, practical planning, and persistent effort form the heart of Hill's approach to achievement.

Think and Grow Rich presents a system for turning a strong desire into a practical goal. Hill stresses clear aims, belief, planning, specialized knowledge, and steady action. He also argues that cooperation with people who share your purpose strengthens your efforts. His approach places great weight on mental habits and persistence when plans fail. Treat these ideas as motivational principles, not as a guarantee of wealth or a substitute for sound financial decisions.

Think and Grow Rich at a glance

Main idea

A clear purpose, supported by planning, knowledge, cooperation, and persistence, gives ambition a practical direction.

The problem it addresses

Unclear goals, weak planning, hesitation, and repeated surrender can prevent people from making useful progress.

What it promises

Hill offers a sequence of principles for organizing thought and action around a chosen goal, without guaranteeing a particular financial result.

Main themes

  • Desire and Goal Setting. Choosing a definite aim and deciding what work or service will support it
  • Faith and Auto-Suggestion. Using belief and repeated statements to keep a chosen purpose in attention
  • Specialized Knowledge. Seeking useful skills and information instead of relying on general knowledge alone
  • Organized Planning and Decision. Turning a goal into a workable plan and making decisions without needless delay
  • Persistence and the Master Mind. Continuing after setbacks and working with people whose knowledge and aims complement yours

The story of the book

Choose a Definite Aim

Hill begins with the role of desire in achievement. He asks readers to move beyond a vague wish and choose a definite purpose. For a financial goal, this means deciding what amount is sought, what service will be offered in return, and when progress will be reviewed. The purpose is to give daily work a clear direction. Hill connects this process with belief and repeated attention. His claims about the power of thought reflect his motivational philosophy and should not be mistaken for proof that thinking alone produces money.

Build Knowledge and a Plan

The book then connects ambition with knowledge and organized effort. Wanting a result does not replace learning the skills needed to produce it. Readers are encouraged to seek specialized information, make practical plans, and involve people with useful experience. The emphasis is on putting knowledge to work rather than collecting facts without a purpose. Hill also discusses decision-making and warns against allowing hesitation to control every important choice. A sound application of this idea includes checking costs, risks, and available evidence before committing resources.

Work Through Setbacks

Persistence is another central theme. Hill treats defeat as a point at which a person should examine the plan and continue with greater care. This does not mean that every goal is realistic or that continuing at any cost is wise. A useful modern reading distinguishes commitment to a purpose from attachment to one failed method. When results disappoint, review the evidence, identify what went wrong, and change the approach. Persistence becomes productive when it includes learning and adjustment rather than repeating the same actions without thought.

Use Cooperation and Self-Review

Hill also stresses cooperation through what he calls the Master Mind. The idea is that people can combine knowledge, judgment, and effort around a shared aim. His broader discussion includes personal habits, belief, and the management of fear. These themes encourage readers to examine how indecision, criticism, and worry affect their work. The book does not establish that a particular mindset guarantees riches. Its practical value lies in prompting readers to clarify aims, seek advice, plan carefully, and keep reviewing their actions.

About Napoleon Hill

Napoleon Hill was an American author associated with early twentieth-century self-improvement literature. His best-known book, Think and Grow Rich, was published in 1937 during the Great Depression. The Library of Congress identifies the book as an important early work in the self-improvement genre. Hill's writing focuses on ambition, personal initiative, planning, belief, and persistence. He presented these ideas as principles that readers could apply to business and personal goals. His claims about success should be understood within the motivational and historical context in which he wrote.

Hill also wrote The Law of Success, an earlier work that explored personal achievement through a structured series of lessons. The Library of Congress describes Think and Grow Rich as a book that presents thirteen steps related to making money and draws on Hill's stated connection with Andrew Carnegie. Historical accounts of Hill's career and reported encounters should be checked carefully before being repeated as established facts. For readers today, the most useful approach is to study his advice about goal setting and organized effort while evaluating extraordinary claims about wealth and the mind with independent evidence.

6 key lessons from Think and Grow Rich

1. Define a Specific Goal

A broad wish gives daily work little direction. Write down the result you want, the service you will provide, and a date for reviewing progress. Keep the goal specific enough to guide your next action.

Example: At 8 a.m. on Monday, Sarah in Chicago writes a plan to test demand for a neighborhood bakery before signing a lease.

2. Use Repetition to Maintain Focus

Hill recommends repeating a written purpose to keep it active in your mind. Repetition does not make a result certain, but it can remind you of your priorities. Pair each statement with a practical task.

Example: At 7 a.m. in Atlanta, Marcus reads his sales goal and then calls three potential customers before checking social media.

3. Build Specialized Knowledge

General information is not enough when a goal requires a specific skill. Identify what you do not know and learn from training, reliable resources, or experienced people. Apply the new knowledge to a real problem.

Example: Elena spends an hour each evening learning wholesale pricing before preparing a proposal for a Detroit retailer.

4. Make a Workable Plan

A goal becomes more useful when you divide it into actions and deadlines. Check whether the plan fits your resources, then revise it when results show a weakness. Planning is a process, not a promise of success.

Example: On Sunday afternoon, Tom and two colleagues set weekly tasks for their software launch and review progress every Friday.

5. Decide After Checking the Facts

Hill places importance on firm decisions rather than endless hesitation. A sound decision still requires evidence and attention to risk. Set a reasonable deadline, gather essential information, and act when the choice is sufficiently clear.

Example: David reviews a shop lease, estimates monthly costs, and asks an accountant to check the figures before deciding on Thursday.

6. Persist and Adjust

A setback can reveal a flaw in your plan, offer, or timing. Review what happened before trying again. Persistence is useful when it includes learning, realistic limits, and changes that improve your next attempt.

Example: After several clothing shops reject her catalog, Hannah in Miami asks buyers for feedback and changes her product range before sending another offer.

What to remember

  1. A definite purpose gives daily work a clear direction.
  2. Belief and repeated attention are motivational tools, not guarantees of wealth.
  3. Specialized knowledge matters when it is applied to a practical goal.
  4. Organized planning turns ambition into steps that can be reviewed.
  5. Cooperation brings different skills and viewpoints into a shared effort.
  6. Persistence works best when setbacks lead to learning and revision.

The big idea

Hill's approach links a clear purpose with knowledge, planning, cooperation, and continued effort. Its practical value depends on testing ideas against real conditions rather than treating motivation as a guarantee of financial success.

Common mistakes

  • Treating strong desire as a replacement for skills, research, budgeting, and action.
  • Continuing a failing plan without examining the evidence or considering a better approach.

Try it yourself

Write a Definite Goal (15 minutes)

  1. Write down the result you want to achieve in plain language.
  2. Describe the service, work, or effort you will provide in return.
  3. Set a realistic date for reviewing progress and list the first three actions.
  4. Read the plan each morning and complete one action before reviewing distractions.

Build a Knowledge Plan (30 minutes)

  1. List the three skills or facts most important to your goal.
  2. Find a reliable source or a knowledgeable person for each gap.
  3. Schedule time to study and apply one new idea this week.
  4. Record the result and decide what to learn next.

Review a Setback (20 minutes)

  1. Describe one recent setback without blaming yourself or other people.
  2. Separate facts you know from guesses about what caused the problem.
  3. Choose one change to test in your next attempt.
  4. Set a date to review the result and decide whether to continue or adjust.

Should you read Think and Grow Rich?

Read it if

Readers interested in goal setting, personal initiative, and the history of motivational writing may find this book useful. Entrepreneurs and professionals can use its planning and persistence themes as prompts for reviewing their work. Readers should assess its claims about wealth critically.

You may skip it if

Readers seeking modern investment analysis, detailed budgeting methods, or scientific evidence for every psychological claim should use other resources alongside this book. Some of its language reflects the period in which it was written.

Get the full book

Choose a complete edition if you want to read Hill's full argument, chapter sequence, and historical examples rather than a brief summary. A complete edition provides Hill's extended discussion of the thirteen principles, his examples, and his original instructions in context.
