Better Than Before

by Gretchen Rubin · Habits · Mini book

Lasting habits begin when you stop copying other people's routines and start understanding your own nature.

Better Than Before explores why people struggle to change their daily behavior, even when they genuinely want to improve. Gretchen Rubin argues that habits become easier to maintain when they fit our individual needs, personalities, and circumstances. She examines different approaches to forming routines, managing temptation, and responding to expectations. Instead of searching for one perfect method, readers learn to identify the strategies that suit them. The central message is simple: lasting change depends on understanding how you actually behave.

The story of the book

Why change feels difficult

Rubin begins with a familiar problem: people want to improve, yet their good intentions often disappear. A plan that works beautifully for one person may fail another. The difference is not always discipline. People respond differently to rules, rewards, schedules, and social pressure. Understanding those differences provides a more useful starting point than blaming yourself.

Understand your own tendencies

The book examines how people meet inner expectations, such as personal goals, and outer expectations, such as promises to others. Rubin's Four Tendencies framework describes Upholders, Questioners, Obligers, and Rebels according to their responses to these expectations. Each tendency suggests different ways of approaching change. The point is to notice your usual behavior and choose strategies accordingly.

Design the conditions for success

Rubin then examines practical strategies for changing everyday behavior. Monitoring reveals what is happening. Scheduling gives actions a place in the day. Accountability creates a reason to follow through. Convenience makes desired behavior easier, while removing convenience can interrupt unwanted behavior. Abstaining and moderation also suit different people in different situations. No single tactic needs to work for everyone.

Build a personal system

The book brings these ideas together around a central question: which conditions support your own consistency? A reader who responds to deadlines might arrange regular check-ins. Someone who resists rigid rules might experiment with a different structure. Rather than treating a failed strategy as proof of personal weakness, readers can study the failure and adjust their approach. Habit change becomes a process of learning about yourself.

About Gretchen Rubin

Gretchen Rubin is an author who writes about happiness, human nature, habits, and everyday behavior. Her books include The Happiness Project, Better Than Before, and The Four Tendencies. Her work combines personal observation, research, and practical suggestions for changing ordinary routines. She is also known for exploring how differences between people affect their experiences of happiness and self-management.

Rubin hosts the Happier with Gretchen Rubin podcast, where she discusses practical approaches to happier living with her sister, Elizabeth Craft. Her work frequently focuses on small actions and the conditions that make them easier to repeat. In Better Than Before, she applies this interest to habits, arguing that self-knowledge matters when selecting methods for lasting change. Her approach encourages readers to examine their own patterns instead of assuming that one system will suit everyone.

6 key lessons from Better Than Before

1. Understand your tendency

Rubin's Four Tendencies framework describes how people respond to inner and outer expectations. Upholders generally respond to both, Questioners require reasons, Obligers respond more readily to outside expectations, and Rebels resist both. Recognizing your usual response gives you a starting point for choosing a workable habit strategy.

Example: Priya keeps appointments with her running partner but ignores her private exercise goals. She schedules three runs with a friend and uses the commitment to make exercise more consistent.

2. Monitor actual behavior

Memory and intention do not always reflect what happens in daily life. Monitoring means recording behavior so patterns become visible. Instead of guessing why a routine fails, examine when you follow through, when you skip it, and what conditions surround each result.

Example: Rohan believes he checks social media only during breaks. He records each check for a week and discovers that most happen when he gets stuck on difficult work.

3. Make good behavior convenient

Small practical barriers often interrupt good intentions. Reducing the steps required to begin a habit makes action easier. Prepare the environment so the desired behavior is ready when you need it, rather than relying on motivation to overcome unnecessary friction.

Example: Meera wants to read before bed. She places her current book beside her pillow and charges her phone across the room.

4. Schedule the behavior

A clear place in your routine removes repeated decisions about when to act. Scheduling works especially well when an action benefits from regular timing. The goal is not to fill every minute but to give an important behavior a dependable opportunity.

Example: Arjun plans to study after dinner but keeps postponing it. He reserves the first half hour after dinner for study before opening his entertainment apps.

5. Use accountability that fits

Responsibility to another person supports some people more than private promises do. The useful form depends on the individual. Choose accountability that encourages follow-through without creating needless pressure or turning every missed action into a source of shame.

Example: Nisha struggles to practise speaking English alone. She arranges a short weekly conversation with a friend and prepares a topic before each call.

6. Choose your temptation strategy

People differ in how they manage enjoyable but unwanted behavior. Rubin explores both abstaining and moderation rather than insisting on one universal rule. Notice whether a clear boundary or a limited allowance makes a particular temptation easier to handle.

Example: Kabir repeatedly watches one more episode after midnight. He tries stopping after a fixed number of episodes, then compares that approach with avoiding television on work nights.

What to remember

  1. A habit strategy must fit the person using it.
  2. Your response to expectations affects your approach to change.
  3. Track behavior before guessing why it fails.
  4. Make useful actions easier to begin.
  5. Give important routines a dependable place in your day.
  6. Choose temptation rules that match your own behavior.

The big idea

You improve your chances of lasting change when you build habits around your actual nature and circumstances.

Common mistakes

  • Copying a popular routine without checking whether its structure fits your needs.
  • Treating every failed attempt as a lack of discipline instead of examining the strategy and circumstances.

Try it yourself

Observe one habit (10 minutes daily)

  1. Choose one behavior you want to understand.
  2. Record each time it happens for seven days.
  3. Note the time, location, and situation surrounding it.
  4. Use the pattern to choose one change.

Design a convenient routine (15 minutes)

  1. Choose a useful behavior you often postpone.
  2. List the steps needed to begin it.
  3. Remove one unnecessary step or barrier.
  4. Prepare the required materials before the next opportunity.

Test an accountability method (15 minutes to set up)

  1. Choose a habit that benefits from outside support.
  2. Ask a suitable person to check in with you.
  3. Agree on a clear action and review time.
  4. After a week, decide whether the arrangement helped.
