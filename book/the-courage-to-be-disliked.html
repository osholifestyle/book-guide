The Courage to Be Disliked

by Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga · Self-Improvement · Mini book

True freedom begins when you separate your tasks from others and let go of their approval.

The Courage to Be Disliked presents Adlerian psychology through a dialogue between a philosopher and a young man. The book questions whether past experiences must determine a person's future. It examines social comparison, approval-seeking, and the separation of personal responsibilities. The authors argue that contribution to the community matters more than winning recognition. They also encourage readers to focus on present actions. These are philosophical arguments, not proof that trauma has no effects or that every difficulty is simply a choice.

The story of the book

Question the Power of the Past

The book begins with a conversation between a skeptical young man and a philosopher who explains Adlerian psychology. The philosopher challenges the belief that past events must determine every future action. He focuses instead on the purposes that present behavior may serve. This argument is deliberately provocative. It encourages readers to examine whether a familiar response is the only available option. It does not establish that painful experiences have no effects. Trauma and other past events can influence people, and the book's broad claims should be considered critically.

Examine Comparison and Rivalry

The second night explores feelings of inferiority and the role of relationships in human problems. The discussion distinguishes recognizing a weakness from treating life as a contest against other people. Constant comparison can turn colleagues, friends, and family members into rivals. The authors encourage a different approach in which people work toward improvement without needing to defeat others. This does not mean that every disagreement has one cause. It offers a framework for examining how competition and the desire to appear superior affect personal relationships.

Separate Responsibilities

The third night develops the idea of separating tasks. The central question is whose responsibility a decision is and who will face its consequences. You can offer advice, support, or information, but you cannot make every choice for another adult. The same principle protects your own decisions from being controlled by other people's expectations. Task separation does not mean ignoring the effects of your actions on others. It asks you to distinguish genuine responsibility from attempts to control someone else's behavior or secure their approval.

Contribute and Live in the Present

The final discussions focus on community feeling, contribution, and present action. The authors argue that a sense of belonging grows when people see themselves as contributors rather than competitors. They also challenge the habit of postponing life until a distant goal is achieved. Their view encourages readers to give attention to what they are doing now. Contribution does not require abandoning personal boundaries or seeking no recognition under every circumstance. The practical point is to make meaningful choices without making every action depend on praise or future success.

About Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga

Ichiro Kishimi is a Japanese philosopher and author associated with Adlerian psychology. According to the publisher, he was born in Kyoto, where he continues to live and work. He writes and lectures on Adlerian ideas and provides counseling for young people in psychiatric clinics. He has also translated selected writings by Alfred Adler into Japanese. His role in The Courage to Be Disliked is to present Adlerian thought through a philosophical dialogue that invites readers to question common beliefs about happiness, relationships, and personal responsibility.

Fumitake Koga is a Japanese professional writer who has worked on business and general nonfiction. The publisher explains that he encountered Adlerian psychology as a young adult and later visited Kishimi in Kyoto to discuss its ideas. The two authors shaped those discussions into a dialogue between a philosopher and a skeptical young man. This structure allows readers to encounter objections alongside the philosopher's answers. 6 key lessons from The Courage to Be Disliked

1. Examine Your Present Aim

The book asks readers to consider what a current behavior achieves, rather than assuming that the past fully explains it. This question can reveal choices worth changing. It does not mean that past harm has no effect or that every response is deliberate.

Example: At three on Monday afternoon in Osaka, Kenji notices he is postponing exam practice and makes a short study plan instead of assuming failure is inevitable.

2. Separate Your Responsibilities

Distinguish choices that belong to you from decisions that another person must make. Offer help when appropriate, but avoid taking control of another person's responsibilities. This boundary supports independence while still allowing care and cooperation.

Example: At seven on Tuesday evening in Tokyo, Yuki agrees to answer her son's homework questions but leaves the decision to study with him.

3. Question the Need for Approval

Constantly adjusting your life to satisfy other people's expectations can make your decisions less authentic. Consider whose values guide your choice and whether you are avoiding disapproval at the cost of a meaningful goal.

Example: At noon in Kyoto, Taro tells his parents that he intends to study graphic design and explains why he prefers it to accounting.

4. Reduce Unhelpful Rivalry

Competition can turn ordinary differences into threats. You can learn from another person's success without treating it as proof of your own failure. Focus on your own progress and look for ways to cooperate when interests allow.

Example: On Tuesday morning in Nagoya, Sora shares a useful customer handover with a new colleague instead of withholding information to protect his ranking.

5. Contribute Without Demanding Praise

Helping a community can be meaningful even when nobody notices. The book encourages readers to consider contribution as a source of belonging rather than a way to collect approval. This does not require ignoring fair recognition or personal boundaries.

Example: At four on Friday afternoon in Sapporo, Emi tidies the shared staff kitchen after lunch and leaves the space ready for the next shift.

6. Give Attention to the Present

A distant goal should guide your actions without making the present seem worthless. Focus on the task in front of you and take it seriously. Review future plans when needed, but do not postpone every meaningful experience until success arrives.

Example: At five in the morning in Kyoto, Daiki concentrates on preparing bread for customers rather than spending the whole shift worrying about yesterday's sales.

What to remember

  2. Social comparison can turn relationships into contests over status and worth.
  3. Separating tasks means respecting responsibility, not refusing all help or cooperation.
  4. Freedom from approval-seeking involves making choices based on considered values.
  5. Community contribution offers one way to develop belonging without constant rivalry.
  6. Attention to present action gives long-term goals a practical role in daily life.

The big idea

Kishimi and Koga use Adlerian psychology to question approval-seeking and encourage personal responsibility. Their ideas are best read as a philosophical framework for reflection, not as universal laws that explain every person's suffering.

Common mistakes

  • Interpreting the book's argument as proof that trauma has no lasting psychological effects.
  • Using task separation as an excuse to ignore responsibilities, consequences, or reasonable requests for help.

Try it yourself

Review a Relationship Conflict (15 minutes)

  1. Choose a disagreement that has been occupying your attention.
  2. Write down the decisions that belong to you and those that belong to the other person.
  3. Choose one respectful action that addresses your own responsibility without trying to control the other person's choice.
  4. Review the result and decide whether another conversation is needed.

Examine an Avoided Choice (20 minutes)

  1. Describe a decision you keep postponing and the outcome you want.
  2. List the concerns that make the decision difficult, including fear of disapproval.
  3. Separate realistic risks from assumptions about what other people will think.
  4. Choose a manageable next step and set a time to complete it.

Practice Community Contribution (10 minutes)

  1. Identify one practical need in your family, workplace, or neighborhood.
  2. Choose a small action that respects your time and responsibilities.
  3. Complete the action without making praise the main reason for doing it.
  4. Reflect on whether the action was useful and what you learned about cooperation.

Should you read The Courage to Be Disliked?

Read it if

Readers interested in personal responsibility, social comparison, approval-seeking, and Adlerian psychology may find this book useful. Its dialogue format makes philosophical questions accessible and gives space to common objections. It is especially relevant to readers who want to examine how relationships influence their choices.

You may skip it if

Readers looking for a clinical treatment guide or a research-based explanation of trauma should use specialist sources instead. The dialogue presents strong philosophical claims that some readers will find too sweeping. Its ideas should not replace professional help when serious psychological difficulties require care.
