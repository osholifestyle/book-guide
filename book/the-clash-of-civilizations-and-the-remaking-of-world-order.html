The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order

by Samuel P. Huntington · Politics & Society · Mini book

Explore Huntington's controversial claim that cultural identity would shape international conflict after the Cold War.

Samuel Huntington argues that cultural and religious identities drive international politics after the Cold War. He groups countries into major civilizations and predicts that cultural differences will shape future global conflicts. The book examines shifting Western power, growing Asian influence, and Islamic political movements. Huntington believes global peace depends on respecting cultural boundaries rather than forcing Western values onto other societies. Critics argue that his broad cultural categories overlook economic interests and internal national divisions.

The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order at a glance

Main idea

Huntington argues that cultural and civilizational identities would become major sources of international cooperation and conflict.

The problem it addresses

A world explained mainly through political ideology or economic competition misses the importance Huntington assigns to cultural identity.

What it promises

The book offers a framework for interpreting global power shifts and managing tensions between different civilizational groupings.

What is inside the book

  • Part I: A World of Civilizations. Argues that post-Cold War world politics has become multipolar and multicivilizational, with cultural identity replacing ideological alignment.
  • Part II: The Shifting Balance of Civilizations. Examines the relative decline of Western dominance alongside the demographic expansion of Islam and economic growth across Asia.
  • Part III: The Emerging Order of Civilizations. Explores how countries align around core civilizational states and reconstruct political partnerships based on shared cultural heritage.
  • Part IV: Clashes of Civilizations. Analyzes violent fault-line conflicts along borders where different civilizations meet and the risks of wider global escalation.
  • Part V: The Future of Civilizations. Recommends that Western powers practice mutual restraint and recognize civilizational diversity to maintain future world stability.

The story of the book

A different explanation of world politics

Huntington begins by questioning how international politics should be understood after the Cold War. The old division between communist and capitalist blocs no longer explains every major rivalry. He argues that cultural identity deserves greater attention. In his framework, shared histories, traditions, and religions influence how societies understand themselves and distinguish themselves from others.

Civilizations as political groupings

The book divides the world into broad civilizations, including Western, Islamic, Hindu, Sinic, Japanese, Orthodox, Latin American, and African groupings. These categories are Huntington's analytical framework, not fixed descriptions of every person or country. He argues that cultural ties influence alliances and disputes, particularly when political boundaries overlap with differences in identity. Critics point out that each grouping contains substantial internal disagreement.

Power shifts and Western influence

Huntington examines the changing balance of global power. He argues that Western societies cannot assume their political ideas and institutions will be accepted everywhere as universal models. He pays particular attention to the growth of Asian power and the political importance of Islamic identity. His analysis asks Western leaders to understand the interests and perspectives of other societies rather than assume that Western influence will continue unchecked.

Conflict and coexistence

The final argument concerns how states should respond to cultural differences. Huntington expects some serious conflicts to occur across civilizational boundaries and discusses the dangers of competing claims to power. He recommends greater recognition of cultural differences and restraint in attempts to dominate other societies. His approach remains debated because conflicts also arise within civilizations, while economic interests, political leaders, institutions, and historical circumstances often cross cultural boundaries.

About Samuel P. Huntington

Samuel P. Huntington was an American political scientist and a long-serving professor at Harvard University. His research covered American politics, civil-military relations, political development, and international affairs. He also held a role in national security planning during the Carter administration and helped establish the journal Foreign Policy. His work often examined how political institutions respond to social change and shifts in power.

Huntington first developed his civilization argument in an article published in Foreign Affairs. He later expanded it into this book, adding historical discussion and proposals about the future international order. The book became influential in debates about foreign policy, religion, and global conflict. Its influence does not mean its predictions were universally accepted. Scholars have challenged its broad cultural categories and argued that political and economic causes of conflict require greater attention.

Quick facts

  • The book was published in 1996 by Simon and Schuster.
  • It expands an earlier argument Huntington presented in Foreign Affairs in 1993.
  • Huntington taught at Harvard and directed its John M. Olin Institute for Strategic Studies.

6 key lessons from The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order

1. Identity shapes political choices

Huntington argues that shared cultural identity influences how groups understand allies, rivals, and international events. His framework encourages readers to consider history and religion alongside military or economic interests. It does not establish that culture always determines political behaviour.

Example: At 9:00 AM at the foreign ministry in Athens, an ambassador named Sophia prepares for regional border talks. She reviews religious history alongside trade data to avoid misunderstandings with neighboring delegates.

2. Civilizations are broad categories

Huntington uses civilizations to organise a complicated world into large cultural groupings. These categories highlight patterns, but they also hide differences within countries and communities. Readers should treat the framework as a hypothesis to test, not a perfect map of human identity.

Example: At 2:00 PM at a news desk in Istanbul, a reporter named Tariq interviews local shopkeepers and university professors. Their opinions on foreign policy differ sharply despite sharing the same national background.

3. Power does not stand still

The book stresses that global influence changes as economies, states, and institutions develop. Huntington challenges the assumption that Western countries will permanently dominate international affairs. A careful reader should compare his argument with actual changes in economic and military power.

Example: At 11:00 AM at an electronics firm in Seattle, an export director named Brian reviews surging demand across Asian markets. He updates manufacturing plans rather than expecting foreign buyers to purchase American models.

4. Universal claims need scrutiny

Huntington warns that one society's political values are not accepted as universal truths by other nations. This dynamic challenges Western efforts to export democratic models. While cultural traditions remain open to debate, global policies must respect differing perspectives.

Example: At 4:00 PM at a community center in Nairobi, a field manager named Grace introduces a public healthcare initiative. She adapts administrative guidelines to local customs while maintaining high health standards.

5. Conflict needs more than one explanation

The book foregrounds culture, but readers should also examine material interests, political leadership, inequality, and historical grievances. Cultural difference alone does not prove that a war was inevitable. Testing competing explanations produces a more reliable account of international events.

Example: At 10:30 AM at a university library in Delhi, a researcher named Rohan analyzes a cross-border water dispute. He studies irrigation treaties and crop yields instead of assuming religious differences caused the conflict.

6. Coexistence requires restraint

Huntington argues that global stability requires leaders to recognise cultural differences and avoid unnecessary attempts at domination. Dialogue and mutual restraint become especially important when powerful states disagree. His proposal remains open to debate, particularly over how states should respond to violations of rights.

Example: At 3:30 PM at the peace summit in Geneva, a negotiator named Elena outlines core security concerns for both delegations. She encourages mutual compromise before either nation enacts trade penalties.

What to remember

  1. Cultural identity is one factor in international politics.
  2. Civilizations are broad analytical categories, not uniform communities.
  3. Global economic and military power changes over time.
  4. Different societies do not automatically share the same political assumptions.
  5. Cultural explanations must be tested against political and economic evidence.
  6. Recognising differences does not require accepting every political practice.

The big idea

Huntington asks readers to take cultural identity seriously in international affairs while offering a framework that needs critical testing.

Common mistakes

  • Treating Huntington's civilizational categories as permanent, uniform identities that determine how every country behaves.
  • Assuming every international conflict is cultural while overlooking territory, resources, political leadership, and economic interests.

Try it yourself

Analyse an international dispute (25 minutes)

  1. Choose a documented international dispute.
  2. List its historical, political, economic, and cultural factors.
  3. Identify which factors Huntington's framework explains well.
  4. Compare his explanation with at least one alternative account.

Test a civilization label (20 minutes)

  1. Choose one of Huntington's civilizational groupings.
  2. Select several countries or communities associated with it.
  3. Find examples of political disagreement within the grouping.
  4. Write down what the broad category explains and what it hides.

Compare competing interests (20 minutes)

  1. Choose two countries with different cultural backgrounds.
  2. Find an example of cooperation between them.
  3. Identify the shared interests behind that cooperation.
  4. Explain why cultural difference did not prevent agreement.
