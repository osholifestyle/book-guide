The Color of Law

by Richard Rothstein · Politics & Society · Mini book

Discover how government housing policies helped create and preserve racial segregation across American neighbourhoods.

Richard Rothstein argues that American housing segregation resulted directly from government action rather than private prejudice. Federal, state, and local agencies created segregated neighbourhoods through public housing choices, zoning laws, and discriminatory mortgage rules. These official policies barred Black families from buying suburban homes and building generational wealth. Government-sponsored segregation deepened racial inequalities in schools, jobs, and community resources. Rothstein insists that because public policy created residential segregation, public policy must provide the remedy.

Segregated neighbourhoods are often explained as the result of private choices, overlooking government decisions that restricted housing opportunities.

The story of the book

Segregation was not simply accidental

Rothstein opens by challenging the belief that American neighbourhoods became segregated mainly because people preferred to live among their own racial group. He argues that government action played a much larger role than many accounts acknowledge. Housing rules, public investment, lending arrangements, and local decisions shaped who lived in each community. His central distinction is between segregation enforced or created through government action and separation that develops through private behaviour.

Housing policy shaped opportunity

The book examines public housing, racial zoning, mortgage lending, and the development of suburbs. Rothstein explains how government programmes directed resources toward white households while restricting opportunities for Black families. These policies affected more than where people slept. Homeownership provided a primary route to building wealth, while exclusion from suburban areas restricted access to jobs, schools, transport, and public services.

Private decisions and public power

Rothstein also examines private agreements, discriminatory real estate practices, and government responses to residential change. He argues that private discrimination became powerful because public authorities supported and enforced it. His account challenges explanations that place the entire burden on individual prejudice. It instead asks readers to examine the rules, agencies, financial arrangements, and political decisions that structured people's choices.

Facing the consequences

The final sections consider how segregation produced through government action should be understood and addressed. Rothstein connects residential exclusion with lasting differences in wealth and opportunity. He argues that recognising the government's role matters when considering possible remedies. The book does not suggest that every present-day inequality has one cause. It provides a historical framework for identifying specific policies and examining their continuing effects.

About Richard Rothstein

Richard Rothstein is an American researcher and writer whose work focuses on education, housing, and racial inequality. He has worked as a national education correspondent and written extensively about public policy. His research examines how government decisions shape opportunities for families and communities. He has been associated with the Economic Policy Institute and other organisations concerned with education and civil rights.

Rothstein wrote The Color of Law to challenge accounts of American residential segregation that focus mainly on private prejudice or voluntary choices. He draws on historical records, housing policies, court decisions, and examples from different American cities. The book brought renewed attention to the role of federal, state, and local authorities in creating segregated communities. Its argument has influenced discussions of housing history, civil rights, and potential remedies for past government discrimination.

Quick facts

  • The book was published by Liveright in 2017.
  • Its subtitle is A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America.
  • The book received the 2018 Hillman Prize for Book Journalism and was longlisted for the National Book Award.

6 key lessons from The Color of Law

1. Separate private choice from policy

Rothstein asks readers to distinguish private prejudice from government action that restricted housing choices. The difference matters because the causes of segregation determine which remedies are appropriate. A complete explanation must examine the rules and institutions behind individual decisions.

Example: At 9:30 AM at the municipal archives in St. Louis, a historian named Sarah examines 1940s federal loan maps. She discovers government rules explicitly barred Black buyers from suburban subdivisions.

2. Housing access shapes wealth

The book connects housing discrimination with unequal access to homeownership and the wealth that property provides. Exclusion from mortgage programmes or appreciating neighbourhoods affects families beyond the immediate housing decision. These economic disparities pass directly between generations.

Example: At 2:00 PM at a banking branch in Richmond, David applies for a small business loan backed by his family home equity. Decades earlier, discriminatory federal mortgage guidelines denied his grandfather a suburban home purchase.

3. Public housing reflects public choices

Rothstein examines how decisions about public housing locations and resident eligibility shaped racial separation. Public programmes do not operate outside politics. Their design and implementation either widen exclusion or improve access to housing and opportunity.

Example: At 11:00 AM at the city planning hall in Detroit, an architect named Marcus attends a public housing hearing. He points out that council proposals concentrate all low-income high-rises in a single industrial district.

4. Suburban growth has a history

The book challenges the idea that suburban racial patterns emerged solely through ordinary market choices. Government-backed finance, planning, and local rules helped shape where housing was built and who could purchase it. Understanding those decisions makes the history of suburban inequality clearer.

Example: At 4:00 PM in Levittown, an urban planning student named Emily reviews original 1947 property deeds. She finds explicit clauses supported by federal guarantees that restricted home sales to white families.

5. Institutions enforce exclusion

Rothstein shows how separate public and private institutions work together to limit opportunity. Housing authorities, mortgage lenders, local courts, and real estate brokers reinforce one another. Examining each institution reveals how systemic exclusion persists across generations.

Example: At 1:00 PM at a real estate agency in Atlanta, a housing inspector named Jamal examines mortgage denial files. He uncovers coordinated practices between private brokers and local banks that steer buyers by race.

6. Remedies need historical evidence

The book argues that government responsibility must be understood before possible remedies are considered. Different policies produced different harms, so remedies require evidence about what happened and who was affected. Historical recognition is a starting point, not a substitute for practical policy design.

Example: At 10:00 AM at the civic center in Evanston, a council member named Laura presents historical land records. The city council uses the archival evidence to fund housing grants for affected residents.

What to remember

  1. Residential segregation was shaped by government decisions.
  2. Private prejudice and public policy are not the same thing.
  3. Housing exclusion restricts wealth building across generations.
  4. Public housing choices affect neighbourhood opportunities.
  5. Suburban development has a political and financial history.
  6. Historical evidence matters when designing remedies.

The big idea

Rothstein argues that understanding American residential segregation requires confronting the government policies that helped create it.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the book argues that private prejudice never contributed to segregation.
  • Treating all present-day housing inequality as the direct result of one policy or one historical event.

Try it yourself

Trace a housing policy (25 minutes)

  1. Choose an American city and a documented housing policy.
  2. Find the original policy or a reliable historical account.
  3. Identify who qualified for its benefits and who faced restrictions.
  4. Explain how the policy shaped residential patterns.

Examine neighbourhood opportunity (25 minutes)

  1. Choose two neighbourhoods in the same metropolitan area.
  2. Compare available information about housing, transport, schools, and employment access.
  3. Research historical planning or lending decisions affecting each area.
  4. Separate documented causes from assumptions about residents' choices.

Evaluate a proposed remedy (20 minutes)

  1. Find a documented proposal addressing housing discrimination or segregation.
  2. Identify the historical problem it intends to address.
  3. List the evidence needed to judge whether the proposal works.
  4. Consider possible benefits, limitations, and unintended effects.

Should you read The Color of Law?

Read it if

This book suits readers interested in American history, housing policy, civil rights, and the relationship between government decisions and racial inequality. It is especially useful for people who want to understand why residential segregation cannot be explained through personal choices alone.

You may skip it if

Readers looking for a global history of urban planning should begin with a broader comparative work. Those seeking current local housing statistics or legal advice will need additional, up-to-date sources.

Get the full book

Buy the full book if you want its historical examples, legal discussion, and detailed account of government involvement in residential segregation. The full text offers the documented cases and connected historical argument needed to evaluate Rothstein's claims closely.

A reliable summary suffices if you only need the central distinction between government-supported segregation and private discrimination.
