The Spirit Level

by Richard Wilkinson and Kate Pickett · Politics & Society · Mini book

Discover why Wilkinson and Pickett argue that the gap between rich and poor affects the wellbeing of an entire society.

Richard Wilkinson and Kate Pickett argue that large income gaps harm entire societies. They compare wealthy nations across health, education, violence, and trust. Their central finding shows that more equal countries perform better on nearly every social measure. The authors explain that wide inequality fuels status anxiety and social stress, damaging community relationships. They urge public policies that reduce income gaps and build cooperative communities.

The Spirit Level at a glance

Main idea

The authors argue that more equal societies tend to experience better health and social outcomes than less equal ones.

The problem it addresses

A society becomes materially wealthy while still experiencing serious problems linked to social divisions and unequal status.

What it promises

The book offers a research-based case for treating economic equality as a factor in collective wellbeing.

What is inside the book

  • Part I: Material Success, Social Failure. Explains why economic growth alone no longer improves wellbeing in rich nations and how status inequality creates social stress.
  • Part II: The Costs of Inequality. Examines statistical evidence linking wider income gaps with health problems, lower trust, violence, imprisonment, and reduced social mobility.
  • Part III: A Better Society. Proposes building more cooperative and sustainable societies through employee ownership, narrower salary differentials, and community trust.

The story of the book

Wealth is not the whole story

Wilkinson and Pickett begin by questioning whether rising national wealth automatically produces healthier and happier societies. They compare rich countries using data on health and social outcomes. Their argument is that differences in income distribution matter alongside overall prosperity. A wealthy country still experiences serious problems if the distance between its richest and poorest people is large.

Poverty and inequality are different

The authors distinguish having too little to meet basic needs from living in a society with large differences in income and status. They argue that relative position affects how people see themselves and others. When social rank becomes more important, people experience greater insecurity and pressure to prove their worth. The authors connect these pressures with several social and health outcomes.

A wide range of social effects

The middle sections examine community relationships, mental health, physical health, obesity, education, teenage births, violence, imprisonment, and social mobility. The authors use comparisons and statistical relationships to support their case. They argue that inequality affects the wider population, not only people on low incomes. Critics have questioned some methods, the strength of particular relationships, and whether the data establish the causes proposed.

A different social priority

The final sections consider what more equal societies look like. Wilkinson and Pickett connect equality with stronger community life, cooperation, and environmental sustainability. They challenge the idea that constant competition is the only realistic way to organise human life. Their proposal is not simply to increase everyone's income, but to reduce the social distance that makes status and rank so powerful.

About Richard Wilkinson and Kate Pickett

Richard Wilkinson is a British social epidemiologist whose work examines income distribution and health. He studied economic history before training in epidemiology, the study of health patterns across populations. His research helped establish income inequality as an important subject in debates about public health and social policy. He later became Professor Emeritus at the University of Nottingham Medical School and held an honorary professorship at University College London.

Kate Pickett is a British epidemiologist and social scientist whose research focuses on health, social inequality, and wellbeing. She studied physical anthropology and nutritional sciences before training in epidemiology. She became a professor at the University of York and worked on research into how social conditions affect health. Wilkinson and Pickett co-founded the Equality Trust, an organisation that promotes public understanding of economic inequality and its possible social effects.

Quick facts

  • The book was first published in 2009 by Allen Lane.
  • Its subtitle is Why Greater Equality Makes Societies Stronger in the American edition.
  • The authors examine a range of health and social outcomes using comparisons between richer countries.

6 key lessons from The Spirit Level

1. Separate poverty from inequality

Poverty concerns whether people have enough resources to meet basic needs. Inequality concerns how resources are distributed across a society. The authors argue that even when basic living standards rise, large income gaps still influence social relationships and people's sense of status.

Example: At 8:00 AM at the municipal hall in Oxford, a city planner named Daniel reviews regional income figures. He notes that while local poverty rates remain low, wide salary gaps leave service workers feeling excluded.

2. Look beyond national averages

A country's overall wealth does not reveal how evenly income is shared or how different groups experience daily life. Wilkinson and Pickett compare social outcomes alongside income distribution. Their approach encourages readers to ask who benefits from prosperity rather than relying on a single average.

Example: At 1:30 PM at a housing clinic in Manchester, a social worker named Priya examines neighbourhood rent statistics. She finds that rising citywide averages disguise heavy financial stress among working families.

3. Status creates social pressure

The authors argue that wide income gaps heighten social rank and competitive pressure. People worry constantly about social judgment when status differences become obvious. This daily status competition increases psychological stress across all income levels.

Example: At 6:00 PM in an accounting office in Bristol, a junior auditor named Arjun feels anxious during a team dinner. He worries about his older car after coworkers spend the evening discussing luxury purchases.

4. Social trust matters

The book examines relationships between inequality and trust in other people. When communities feel sharply divided, cooperation becomes harder and people feel disconnected from strangers. The authors treat trust as an important part of social wellbeing rather than a minor personal preference.

Example: At 10:00 AM at the community garden in Leeds, a neighborhood volunteer named Clara organizes a weekend cleanup. She introduces longtime residents to recent arrivals to rebuild local cooperation and trust.

5. Health has social conditions

Wilkinson and Pickett connect social conditions with health outcomes instead of explaining health only through individual choices. Their analysis asks whether insecurity, status pressure, and unequal resources affect behaviour and wellbeing. Individual circumstances still matter, and statistical associations need careful interpretation.

Example: At 11:00 AM at a family clinic in Sheffield, Dr. Thomas investigates high hypertension rates among transit drivers. He studies irregular shift patterns and workplace stress alongside their daily dietary habits.

6. Cooperation is a social goal

The authors argue for societies that place greater value on cooperation and shared wellbeing. They question whether status competition and consumption should dominate social life. Their proposal encourages public debate about the distribution of resources and the institutions that shape everyday relationships.

Example: At 7:00 PM at a recreation center in Newcastle, a program director named Hannah convenes a neighborhood board. The council funds affordable sports clubs rather than expensive private fitness facilities.

What to remember

  1. Poverty and inequality describe different social problems.
  2. Average national wealth hides differences in income distribution.
  3. Status competition fuels stress and harms social relationships.
  4. Trust and community life are important measures of wellbeing.
  5. Statistical links do not automatically prove causation.
  6. The authors argue for greater equality and stronger cooperation.

The big idea

The Spirit Level argues that the distance between rich and poor matters because social inequality affects the wellbeing of entire communities.

Common mistakes

  • Claiming that every social problem is caused by inequality or that the book proves every proposed causal link.
  • Assuming the authors argue that economic growth is useless or that every country reaches the same outcomes through identical policies.

Try it yourself

Compare income measures (25 minutes)

  1. Choose two countries or regions with reliable income data.
  2. Find their average income and a measure of income inequality.
  3. Compare the results without assuming that either measure explains every social outcome.
  4. Write down what additional information you would need to understand the differences.

Map local wellbeing (20 minutes)

  1. Choose a community you know.
  2. List social indicators such as health access, education, trust, and safety.
  3. Identify which groups experience the greatest barriers.
  4. Suggest one change that improves access or reduces exclusion.

Check a statistical claim (20 minutes)

  1. Choose one claim linking inequality to a social outcome.
  2. Find the data and research method behind the claim.
  3. Ask whether the study shows correlation, causation, or a proposed explanation.
  4. Record one limitation and one additional source worth checking.

Should you read The Spirit Level?

Read it if

This book suits readers interested in public health, economics, social policy, and the effects of inequality on everyday life. It is especially useful for readers who want to examine how income distribution shapes relationships and social outcomes.

You may skip it if

Readers who want a basic introduction to economics without statistical comparisons prefer a simpler starting point. Those seeking an uncontested scientific account should also read the book's critics and later research.

Get the full book

Buy the full book if you want to examine its evidence, graphs, research comparisons, and proposals for a more equal society. The complete book provides the comparative evidence, detailed arguments, and discussion of social policy that a short guide cannot reproduce.

A reliable summary suffices if you only need the central thesis and the main social outcomes discussed.
