The Success Principles

by Jack Canfield · Self-Improvement · Mini book

Taking responsibility for your choices, setting clear goals, and acting consistently gives you a practical way to move toward the life you want.

The Success Principles presents practical ideas for turning personal ambitions into clear plans and daily action. Jack Canfield and Janet Switzer stress personal responsibility, goal-setting, and learning from people who have achieved similar results. The book also explores confidence, relationships, teamwork, and money. Its central message is to stop waiting for perfect conditions and begin changing the actions you control. These principles offer a framework for progress, not a guarantee of success.

The story of the book

Start with Responsibility

The book begins with a practical question: what can you change about the situation you are facing? Canfield and Switzer encourage readers to examine their own responses instead of spending all their energy blaming other people or circumstances. This does not mean that people control every event or deserve every hardship. It means identifying the choices that remain available and acting on them.

Make the Goal Clear

The next stage is deciding what you want. A vague wish gives you little guidance when competing demands appear. The authors encourage readers to define their aims, put them into words, and identify steps that move them closer. Learning from people with relevant experience can also reveal useful methods and help readers avoid repeating preventable mistakes.

Turn Plans into Action

Planning matters only when it changes what you do. The book discusses consistent effort, useful habits, learning, and taking action before every uncertainty disappears. Its Rule of Five offers one way to support a major objective through repeated actions. The value of the method lies in directing attention toward useful work, rather than treating a fixed number of actions as a promise of success.

Build Support for Continued Progress

The book also considers the people and systems around a goal. Mentors can offer experience, colleagues can contribute different skills, and supportive groups can encourage accountability. Strong relationships require listening, respect, and honest communication. The wider message is that achievement involves more than personal ambition. It also depends on how people learn, cooperate, manage their time, and respond when plans need to change.

6 key lessons from The Success Principles

1. Focus on Your Response

You cannot control every event, but you can examine how you respond. Look for one action within your control instead of spending the entire day blaming circumstances. Responsibility begins with the next useful choice, not with denying a genuine problem.

Example: At 9 a.m. in Dallas, Arthur learns that a client has cancelled a contract. He reviews the proposal, asks a colleague for feedback, and contacts another potential customer.

2. Define a Specific Goal

A clear goal gives your attention a direction. State what you want, why it matters, and how you will recognize progress. Choose a deadline when appropriate, then identify the first step you can complete without waiting for perfect conditions.

Example: On Sunday evening in Denver, Sarah decides to contact five potential fitness clients each weekday and records her outreach in a simple spreadsheet.

3. Break Work into Actions

Large ambitions often feel difficult because the next step is unclear. Divide a goal into actions you can schedule and complete. The Rule of Five is one approach described in the book for making repeated progress toward an important objective.

Example: Marcus in Atlanta spends Monday morning contacting commercial property managers, updating his proposal, and arranging follow-up meetings for his new service.

4. Ask for Useful Support

You do not need to solve every problem alone. Seek advice from people who have relevant experience, and make your request clear and respectful. A mentor can offer another perspective, but you remain responsible for deciding what advice fits your circumstances.

Example: At 2 p.m. in Chicago, Elena emails a senior colleague and asks for a short meeting to discuss the skills required for promotion.

5. Learn from Setbacks

A disappointing result can provide information about your approach. Separate the outcome from your worth as a person, then examine what needs to change. Keep what worked, correct what did not, and test a better method instead of repeating the same response.

Example: After a presentation goes poorly in Seattle, Tom asks two attendees for specific feedback and revises his opening slides before the next meeting.

6. Build Relationships Deliberately

Progress often depends on trust, communication, and cooperation. Listen without planning your reply, keep your commitments, and acknowledge other people's contributions. Strong relationships do not remove disagreements, but they give people a better basis for resolving them.

Example: During a Thursday meeting in Boston, Brenda lets her teammate finish explaining a delay, then agrees with him on revised deadlines and responsibilities.

What to remember

  1. Focus on the choices you can make instead of blaming every difficult circumstance.
  2. Define what you want clearly enough to decide what to do next.
  3. Turn a major goal into actions that fit your daily schedule.
  4. Use the Rule of Five as a method for supporting consistent progress.
  5. Learn from mentors and colleagues while making your own decisions.
  6. Treat communication, trust, and cooperation as important parts of achievement.

The big idea

A useful approach to success combines responsibility, clear goals, repeated action, continued learning, and supportive relationships. These principles guide decisions, but they cannot guarantee a particular outcome.

Common mistakes

  • Waiting for ideal circumstances before taking a reasonable first step.
  • Confusing written goals or motivational thinking with the daily work needed to pursue them.

Try it yourself

Review Your Response (15 minutes)

  1. Choose one current difficulty at work or in your personal life.
  2. Write down which parts of the situation you control and which parts you do not.
  3. Choose one useful action you can take today and decide when to take it.
  4. Review the result later and identify what you learned.

Plan Your Rule of Five (10 minutes)

  1. Choose one important goal you want to advance this month.
  2. Write five practical actions that would support progress toward that goal.
  3. Schedule the actions across your available workdays.
  4. Review completed actions and adjust the next set to reflect what you learned.

Ask for Expert Feedback (20 minutes)

  1. Identify one skill or problem where another person's experience would be useful.
  2. Choose a person whose experience fits the question.
  3. Send a short, respectful request explaining what advice you need.
  4. Record the most useful suggestion and decide whether to test it.
